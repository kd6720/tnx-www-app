If you manage facilities, run an IT department, or operate anything that still depends on analog phone lines, the FCC just handed you both a problem and an opportunity.

The FCC's POTS forbearance order fundamentally changed the regulatory landscape for Plain Old Telephone Service in the United States. Carriers are no longer required to maintain legacy copper infrastructure under the old rules. That's not theory — it's happening now, and it affects everything from your fire alarm panels to your elevator phones.

Let me break down what actually changed, who needs to pay attention, and how to come out ahead.

## What the FCC actually did

The FCC issued forbearance from certain Title II common-carrier regulations that previously required incumbent local exchange carriers (ILECs) to maintain their copper networks indefinitely. The short version: carriers can now sunset legacy copper services, including analog POTS lines, without seeking case-by-case regulatory approval.

This didn't happen overnight. The FCC telegraphed this shift for years, conducting multiple proceedings with thousands of public comments. The decision reflects an undeniable reality — maintaining two parallel networks (copper and fiber) indefinitely makes zero economic sense.

The forbearance order allows carriers to discontinue legacy services provided they meet notice requirements and offer reasonable transition periods. It doesn't force anyone to rip out working lines tomorrow, but it does mean the clock is ticking.

## Who this hits first

Not every business feels this equally. If you're running VoIP phones over a fiber connection, nothing changes for you. But if your organization relies on analog lines for any of the following, this is your wake-up call:

**Fire alarm panels.** Most existing fire alarm communicators use two dedicated POTS lines. NFPA 72 still permits them, but the copper infrastructure underneath is going away.

**Elevator phones.** ASME A17.1 and building codes require operational emergency phones in elevators. The vast majority still run on analog lines.

**Security systems.** Burglar alarm panels, access control dialers, and duress alarms often use POTS as primary or backup communication.

**Fax machines.** Healthcare, legal, and government still send millions of faxes over analog lines. Yes, in 2026.

**Legacy PBX systems.** If your phone system connects to the outside world via analog trunks or PRI circuits, those circuits have an expiration date.

## What this means in practice

Carriers are already sending disconnection notices for POTS lines in many markets. The timeline varies by region and carrier, but the direction is universal.

The practical impact is straightforward: when a carrier discontinues copper service to a building, every device on those lines goes dark. Fire alarm panels show trouble signals. Elevator phones stop working. Fax machines sit silent. Your PBX loses dial tone.

None of this is theoretical. We see it weekly — facility managers getting 90-day notices with no plan in place.

## Your move: options ranked by urgency

### 1. Audit what you have (do this week)
Walk every building. Inventory every device that connects to a phone line. You'll almost certainly find things you forgot about — old modems, backup lines, rooftop HVAC controllers. Write it all down.

### 2. Identify your compliance dependencies
Fire alarm, elevator, and security system requirements come from codes — NFPA 72, ASME A17.1, NFPA 70, UL 864 — and your AHJ (Authority Having Jurisdiction) has the final say. Find out what your local fire marshal accepts before you buy anything.

### 3. Evaluate replacement technologies
Cellular communicators. IP-based alarm transmission. Hosted voice with LTE failover. Wireless POTS replacement devices that emulate analog service over cellular. Multiple paths exist — the right one depends on your specific devices and compliance requirements.

### 4. Don't wait for the notice
The worst time to solve this problem is after the carrier sends the letter. Lead times for equipment, permitting, and installation can run 6-12 weeks depending on complexity. Start now and you control the timeline. Wait for the notice and someone else does.

## The silver lining

Here's what nobody talks about: most organizations that migrate off POTS end up with better service at lower cost.

A single analog line often runs $65-95/month before taxes and fees. A cellular POTS replacement device typically costs $25-40/month per line. Voice quality is comparable or better. Monitoring capabilities improve. And you never have to worry about a backhoe taking out your copper again.

The forbearance isn't a disaster — it's a forcing function. The organizations that act now will have more reliable communications and lower bills a year from now. The ones that wait will be scrambling.
