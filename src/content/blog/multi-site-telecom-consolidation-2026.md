The "one vendor vs many" debate usually starts with the wrong question. People ask "which carrier should win?" when they should be asking "what is my team spending its time on?"

That's because vendor sprawl isn't primarily a cost problem. It's an operational one.

Every carrier you add brings another login portal, another renewal date, another support queue, another invoice format. A company running 40 locations on 11 carriers isn't just paying 11 bills. They're paying someone on their team to chase those 11 bills every month, reconcile 11 different tax and fee structures, and escalate 11 different support queues.

We've walked into multi-site customers and found telecom invoices nobody had reconciled in months — circuits still being billed years after a location closed, legacy rates that were "grandfathered" into auto-renewal. That's the sprawl tax, and it compounds.

## Consolidation is a sequencing problem, not a vendor problem

Here's what trips people up: they try to consolidate transport — the actual circuits — first, when they should consolidate the management layer first.

The order that actually works:

**Step 1 — Audit what you have.** Map every circuit, every carrier, every contract, every renewal date. You'll find things you didn't know you were paying for. This step is non-negotiable.

**Step 2 — Categorize your sites.** Not every location deserves the same treatment. Tier 1 — HQ, data-heavy, revenue-critical — gets premium redundant connectivity. Tier 2 branch offices get a solid primary plus failover. Tier 3 — alarm panels, elevators, low-bandwidth closets — get compliance-grade but lean connectivity. Treating every site the same is how you overpay.

**Step 3 — Consolidate the management layer.** Get every site onto one dashboard, one support workflow, one escalation path — regardless of how many underlying carriers remain. This is where the real time savings come from.

**Step 4 — Consolidate transport site-by-site.** Only after the first three steps. Move circuits as contracts expire, not all at once. You turn a risky big-bang migration into a controlled rollout.

## The "single throat to choke" trap

The strongest argument for one vendor is also the strongest argument against it.

One vendor means one number to call when something breaks. It also means one infrastructure to fail. We've watched forced consolidations create regional outages where a carrier issue took down dozens of locations at once, because every site depended on the same underlying network.

The fix isn't to avoid consolidation. It's to separate the *management* relationship from the *transport* diversity.

You want a single management layer — one partner who owns the outcome — sitting on top of carrier-diverse circuits. Your backup path should run on entirely different infrastructure than your primary. That way you get the single-throat-to-choke accountability without the single-point-of-failure risk.

## What it looks like in practice

One multi-state property manager we work with went from 14 carriers across 80 properties down to two primary carriers — but kept the whole footprint behind one managed partner. Monthly invoice reconciliation dropped from three days to three hours. More importantly, when one of their primary carriers had a regional fiber cut last winter, every affected site failed over to a diverse path automatically, and their residents never noticed.

That's the goal state: fewer vendors, less chaos, zero single points of failure.

## When you shouldn't consolidate

A patchwork is sometimes the right answer:

- **Geographic coverage gaps.** No carrier covers everywhere well. Forcing a national into a market they don't serve natively means inflated resale rates and worse reliability.
- **Acquisition-heavy organizations.** If you're buying companies faster than you can integrate them, chasing consolidation becomes a treadmill.
- **Pricing leverage.** A second bidder in the room keeps renewal pricing honest. Single-vendor lock-in is how you end up paying 2019 rates in 2026.

## The bottom line

Consolidation isn't about getting down to one carrier. It's about getting to intentional design instead of organic drift.

Start with an audit. Categorize your sites. Consolidate the management layer before the transport. Keep your backup paths diverse. And if you're not sure where to begin, get someone who's done this before to map what you actually have against what you actually need.
