## The Copper Network Is Ending — And It's Happening Faster Than You Think

For over a century, Plain Old Telephone Service (POTS) lines have been the backbone of business communication. These copper lines connected everything — desk phones, fire alarms, elevator emergency phones, building security panels, fax machines, and point-of-sale terminals. When you needed something that *just worked*, copper was the answer.

But the ground is shifting beneath those copper circuits. Major carriers have announced aggressive timelines for retiring their copper infrastructure, and the pace is accelerating. If your business still relies on POTS lines, the window for a smooth, planned migration is closing.

## What's Driving the Copper Sunset

Three forces are converging to end the copper era:

**FCC Order 19-72** gave carriers a formal pathway to retire legacy copper services, provided they offer equivalent alternatives. This wasn't the start of copper retirement — it was the regulatory green light for what carriers were already planning.

**Carrier economics** make copper untenable. Maintaining a parallel physical network of aging copper alongside modern fiber and wireless infrastructure costs carriers billions. AT&T alone has publicly stated its intent to retire the vast majority of its copper footprint. CenturyLink (now Lumen) and Verizon are following similar trajectories.

**Supply chain reality** is the quiet force multiplier. Replacement parts for copper infrastructure — line cards, switching equipment, specialized cable — are increasingly difficult to source. When a copper line fails today, carriers often can't repair it because the parts simply don't exist anymore.

## The Real Cost of Waiting

If your business operates across multiple locations — property management, senior living facilities, hospitality, retail, healthcare — the cost exposure is substantial. Here's what's at stake:

- **Line cost inflation**: As carriers deprioritize copper maintenance, remaining POTS lines become premium-priced. Many organizations are seeing 15–25% annual increases on copper line items that were flat for decades.
- **Downtime risk**: When a copper circuit fails and can't be repaired, you may face weeks of service interruption — not hours. Elevators, fire alarms, and security systems with no dial tone are compliance violations, not inconveniences.
- **Compliance exposure**: NFPA 72 (National Fire Alarm Code) and UL 864 require reliable communication paths for fire alarm monitoring. ASME A17.1 mandates working emergency phones in elevators. A non-functional POTS line behind any of these systems puts your organization at regulatory and legal risk.

## What Successful Migration Looks Like

Organizations that migrate proactively see a very different outcome. A multi-site property management group TrustedNetworx worked with was facing 300+ copper POTS lines across 47 properties, with annual costs climbing past $400,000. After a structured migration to cellular and VoIP alternatives, they reduced their telecom spend by 52% while gaining remote monitoring, failover redundancy, and centralized line management — capabilities copper could never offer.

A senior living operator with 12 facilities faced a different challenge: copper-dependent nurse call systems that couldn't be easily replaced. They needed a migration path that preserved their existing infrastructure investments. The solution: a wireless POTS replacement that emulated copper dial tone without touching their nurse call hardware. Compliance maintained, costs reduced, zero operational disruption.

## The Compliance Checklist

Before you migrate, here's what needs attention:

- **Fire alarm panels**: Confirm your FACP is compatible with cellular or IP communicators. Many modern panels support dual-path communication — cellular primary with IP backup, or vice versa.
- **Elevator phones**: ASME A17.1 (2019 edition and later) permits wireless alternatives, but your AHJ (Authority Having Jurisdiction) may have specific requirements. Verify before cutting copper.
- **Building security**: Alarm panels, gate phones, and access control systems often ride on dedicated POTS lines. Test any replacement solution thoroughly during and after migration.
- **Elevator recall and monitoring**: Some elevator systems use POTS lines for more than just the emergency phone. Audit each elevator's wiring before making changes.

## Don't Wait for the Disconnect Notice

The worst time to plan a POTS migration is after you receive a carrier disconnect notice. At that point, your timeline is dictated by the carrier — often 60 to 90 days — and your options narrow dramatically.

A proactive approach — audit your lines, identify dependencies, build a migration roadmap, and execute in phases — gives you control over the timeline, the budget, and the outcome.
