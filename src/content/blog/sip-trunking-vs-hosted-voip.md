Both options kill your legacy phone bill. The mistake most businesses make is treating them as the same decision. They're not. SIP trunking and hosted VoIP look similar on a quote sheet but solve different problems — and picking the wrong one means paying for an architecture you don't need.

Here's the difference in plain terms, and how to decide.

## The one-sentence difference

SIP trunking connects your existing on-premises phone system to the public phone network over the internet. You keep your hardware — the PBX, the handsets, the call flows, the internal wiring. You just swap the expensive analog or PRI circuits for an internet connection.

Hosted VoIP moves the whole system to the cloud. No PBX in the closet. The provider runs the software, and your team uses desk phones, softphones, or a mobile app.

Same goal — cheaper, more capable calling. Opposite starting points.

## When SIP trunking is the right call

SIP trunking makes sense when you've already made a real investment in a modern-ish PBX and it still works. If the hardware isn't at end of life and your team knows how to run it, you don't have to rip it out to modernize.

The main reasons to choose SIP trunking:

- You bought an IP-capable PBX in the last several years and it's paid off.
- You have complex, custom call flows that would be expensive to rebuild.
- You want to keep capital on the books and spread the migration.
- You have on-site staff comfortable managing the system.

SIP trunking is also a sensible step for businesses that want to consolidate multiple locations onto a single calling backbone without re-architecting every site at once.

The trade-off: you still own hardware. You still maintain it, patch it, and eventually replace it. SIP trunking buys you time and savings, not freedom from phone-system ownership.

## When hosted VoIP is the right call

Hosted VoIP is the right answer when the hardware is the problem — when the PBX is aging out, the vendor is retiring, or the maintenance contract keeps climbing.

The reasons to go hosted:

- Your PBX is end of life, or the vendor has disappeared.
- You want to stop managing phone hardware entirely.
- You're opening new locations and don't want to buy another PBX.
- You want features the old system can't do — mobile apps, voicemail-to-email, CRM integration, call analytics.

Hosted VoIP trades a capital expense for a predictable per-seat operating cost. For most organizations, that's the cleaner financial story — no repair budget, no parts shelf, no truck rolls.

The trade-off: you're dependent on your internet connection. That's not a reason to avoid hosted VoIP — it's a reason to get the underlying connectivity right first.

## Don't skip the connectivity step

Here's the part most conversations skip. Whether you pick SIP trunking or hosted VoIP, call quality lives and dies on your network.

Both approaches need a connection that can handle voice traffic without jitter and packet loss, and both need a failover path when the primary link drops. A hosted phone system on a single flaky broadband line is worse than the legacy system it replaced.

Before you decide on the phone architecture, answer one question: is your connectivity ready? If it isn't, fix that first. The phone decision gets a lot easier once the network is solid.

## How to decide in one afternoon

Grab three facts:

1. The age and condition of your PBX. If it's past end of life, the decision is basically made.
2. Your current per-line and maintenance costs. If they're high, hosted VoIP will usually win on pure math.
3. Who on your team can manage the system today. If the answer is "nobody," that's your answer.

Most businesses we work with land on hosted VoIP — not because SIP trunking is bad, but because the hardware they're trying to preserve stopped making financial sense years ago. The few that choose SIP trunking are usually running a relatively recent PBX they're not ready to sunset.

Either way, the goal is the same: stop paying for copper and start getting modern calling features at a fraction of the cost.
