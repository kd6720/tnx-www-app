I've watched dozens of companies migrate from legacy phone systems to VoIP. Some pull it off in weeks and wonder why they waited. Others burn six figures, frustrate their teams, and end up with worse call quality than they started with. The difference is never the technology. It's the planning.

Here are the mistakes I see over and over — and how to avoid them.

## Mistake #1: Assuming Your Network Is Ready

VoIP doesn't need much bandwidth. A single call uses about 100 Kbps — trivial for any modern connection. But it needs *consistent* bandwidth. Jitter, packet loss, and latency spikes that your team never notices on email or web browsing will turn a VoIP call into a garbled mess.

Before you sign anything, run a network assessment. Check for latency under load, packet loss during peak hours, and whether your switches and routers support QoS (Quality of Service). If your network gear is more than five years old, budget for replacements. A $300 managed switch that prioritizes voice traffic will save you more frustration than any phone system feature.

## Mistake #2: Picking Features Over Reliability

VoIP providers love to demo flashy features. AI transcription. Video conferencing. CRM integrations. Call analytics dashboards that look like a spaceship cockpit. These are genuinely useful — for about 10% of your team.

The other 90% need three things: clear audio, reliable connectivity, and a phone that works the instant they pick it up. If a vendor spends more time on their feature list than their uptime SLA, walk away. Ask for their five-nines track record. Ask what happens during an internet outage. Ask about failover to cellular. If they don't have crisp answers, they're selling software, not phone service.

## Mistake #3: Underestimating the Human Side

People hate changing how they make phone calls. It sounds minor — it's not. Sales teams have workflows built around their desk phones. Receptionists have muscle memory for transferring calls. Facility managers have memorized which button pages which wing.

The best VoIP migration I've seen included a two-week parallel run: old system still working, new phones on desks, people making test calls. By the time they cut over, everyone had already used the new system. The worst migration I've seen was a Friday-afternoon cutover with no training. Monday morning was chaos.

Budget for training. Budget for a transition period. Assign a floor captain on each team who learns the system first. The technology is the easy part.

## Mistake #4: Treating It as an IT Project

VoIP touches every department. Facilities needs to know where phones go. HR needs new hire provisioning. Sales needs call recording and CRM sync. Compliance needs call logging for regulated industries. If IT is the only department in the room during planning, you'll miss half the requirements.

Bring department leads into the conversation early. Ask what their teams actually do on the phone — not what you assume they do. You'll discover things like "we forward calls to a contractor's cell phone after 5 PM" or "we need a phone in the elevator machine room for code compliance." Those details determine whether the migration succeeds or fails.

## Mistake #5: Ignoring E911 and Compliance

Here's the one that can get you sued. Traditional phone lines are tied to a physical address. When someone dials 911, the dispatcher knows exactly where they are. VoIP phones aren't anchored to a location — they're anchored to an IP address. If you don't configure E911 properly, emergency services go to the wrong building.

This isn't theoretical. It's happened. And it's entirely preventable with proper E911 registration and location tracking for every handset in your organization. If you're in healthcare, education, hospitality, or senior living, this isn't optional — it's a regulatory requirement. Make sure your VoIP provider has a documented E911 compliance process before you sign.

## The Bottom Line

VoIP migration isn't complicated when you do the groundwork: assess your network, prioritize reliability over features, plan the human transition, involve every department, and lock down E911 compliance. Skip any of these and you're gambling with your company's communication backbone.
